The model

A binary classifier that estimates the probability a crash ends in a fatality, given the speed zone, weather, vehicle type, location class and year. Trained on a stratified sample of 9,400 crashes with inverse-sampling weights, so its output sits on the true NSW scale where 1.51% of crashes are fatal.

How it was built

01

Features

Speed limit, weather bucket, vehicle group, street lighting, and a location class derived from each crash's coordinates — metro, regional city or rural.

02

Re-weighting

Every fatal crash is in the sample but only a fraction of the others, so rows are weighted — to restore the real class balance.

03

Fitting

75/25 stratified train–test split, on the crashes where street lighting was recorded. Logistic regression, a depth-3 tree and a 300-tree random forest were fitted on identical features.

04

Scoring

Coefficients ship with the page, so predictions run in your browser — no server call and no data leaves the machine.

What drives fatal risk

Odds multiplier per feature, holding everything else fixed. Above 1× raises risk, below 1× lowers it.

A motorcycle as the key traffic unit multiplies the odds of a fatal outcome by about —. Two weather results are worth pausing on: rain lands below 1×, because wet-weather crashes cluster on slower urban roads, and fog barely moves the needle even though 2.3% of fog crashes are fatal — once speed zone and rural location are accounted for, the fog itself adds almost nothing.

Discrimination held-out test set

ROC curve — how well the score separates fatal from non-fatal crashes.

An AUC of — means that if you pick one fatal and one non-fatal crash at random, the model gives the fatal one the higher score that often.

The poster quotes 0.826. That is a different model: the notebook's Random Forest, on the full set of crash attributes, split by year (train 2020–2022, test on unseen 2024). This page ships a logistic regression on twelve readable features with a random 75/25 split, because its coefficients can be shown and it scores in your browser. The Random Forest is the better classifier; this one is the one you can argue with.

Operating point

Fatal crashes are rare, so the decision threshold is a trade: flag more crashes and you catch more deaths but drown in false alarms.

Data. Transport for NSW crash and casualty statistics, 2020–2024: 92,082 crashes, one row per crash — the same records and the same year filter as the Impact Day poster. Map points are a stratified sample of 9,400 of them — every one of the 1,388 fatal crashes plus a proportional sample of the rest — carried in data.js to keep the page light. Every figure taken from that sample is re-weighted back to the full 92,082 before it is shown.

Street lighting. The field is blank on 33,567 of the 92,082 crashes. Those reports are 55% non-casualty and contain 8 fatal crashes between them — 0.02%, against 1.51% overall — so a blank marks a minor report, not a lit road. Including it as a category would let the model score crashes on the paperwork instead of the road, so the model is fitted only on the 58,505 crashes where lighting was recorded, and its baseline rate (2.4%) is that group's, not the state's. Crashes with the field blank still appear on the map; they are scored as if lit, which is the reference level.

The target is death, not all serious harm. Everything on this page models whether a crash was fatal. The same records contain 21,861 people seriously injured against 1,507 killed, and the standard road-safety target — killed or seriously injured, KSI — is 15 times more common than fatality. It also points somewhere else: on deaths, rural roads at 100 km/h and over carry 39% of the harm on 13% of crashes; on KSI, two thirds of the harm sits on urban roads at 70 km/h and under. Speed decides whether a crash kills, not whether it injures. Read the risk figures here as fatality risk specifically.

Limits. The model uses only what these files contain. Driver age, alcohol, seatbelt use, road geometry and emergency response times are absent and would all matter. Coefficients describe associations in historical records, not causes, and should not be used to make decisions about individuals.
